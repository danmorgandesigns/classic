# Trust the Process
## September 17, 2025

I have been deeply into music since I was in elementary school. I was the only Gen X child in a large family of Boomers born in the '50s and 60s, so my early exposure to music ranged from the Beatles through Fleetwood Mac and every '70s rock band in between. I only started discovering music more for my generation sometime in middle school when I got exposed to New Wave and early metal. Think Men at Work and Quiet Riot.

Somewhere along the way I discovered Prog Rock, probably in a tangential way to New Wave, but it certainly came from a back catalog dive into classic Genesis and Yes after I bought the self-titled Genesis album Yes' 90125 both in 1983. Is 12 years old too young to go deep into Prog Rock? Steely Dan got into my veins in my teens too, which is decades ahead of when most people first really dig The Royal Scam.

College exposed me to blues-infused rock in the form of Led Zeppelin and I've never quite recovered. Soon I had a collection of 500 albums and yet never had enough to listen to. Hendrix, SRV... ahh glorious memories of hearing Voodoo Chile for the first time in the original and then in its homage form.

But intriguingly—oddly—I never learned to play an instrument. Left handed guitars were more rare and no roommate who played was left-handed. That's usually the vector, isn't it? A brother, an uncle, a roommate lends you a guitar and you noodle around on it until you're hooked.

This summer I kept seeing [research](https://direct.mit.edu/imag/article/doi/10.1162/IMAG.a.48/131155/Never-too-late-to-start-musical-instrument)* regarding how being a musician or picking up an instrument later in life protects people from cognitive decline. Well, some bit in my brain flipped this summer and I resolved to finally, really, I mean it this time—I'm going to learn to play guitar.

Learning any new motor skill whether it is golf, cross stitch, or guitar, there's an early and steep learning curve to surmount. But with experience and wisdom (No, not age. Stop it.) hopefully one learns patience and to trust the process.

After decades of absorbing music from every direction—family turntables, college dorm stereos, late-night headphone sessions on the floor in a dark room—I’ve realized that listening has been my lifelong apprenticeship. That research about music and cognitive resilience just gave me the nudge to finally put all those years of listening into practice. If sound alone has carried me this far, maybe learning to play will carry me further still—not just in keeping my mind sharp, but in deepening my lifelong relationship with music itself.
